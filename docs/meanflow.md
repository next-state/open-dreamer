## 1. Executive Summary

**MeanFlow** is a generative framework designed to solve the inference latency bottleneck of diffusion and flow-matching models. While traditional Flow Matching (FM) learns an *instantaneous* velocity field $v_t(x)$ requiring numerical integration (10–100 steps) during sampling, MeanFlow learns an **Average Velocity** field $u(x, r, t)$ that maps directly across time intervals.

The core contribution is the **MeanFlow Identity**, a differential equation derived from the fundamental theorem of calculus that relates the average velocity $u$ to the instantaneous velocity $v$. This identity allows the model to be trained to predict the integral of the flow (allowing 1-step generation) while using the instantaneous velocity (known from the FM path) as the ground-truth supervision.

Empirically, MeanFlow achieves state-of-the-art results for one-step generation (e.g., **3.43 FID on ImageNet 256×256** with 1 Function Evaluation) without distillation, pre-training, or curriculum learning.

## 2. Theoretical Framework

### 2.1. The Flow Matching Context
Let $p_0$ be a simple prior (e.g., $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$) and $p_1$ be the data distribution. Flow Matching defines a probability path $p_t$ and a vector field $v_t(x)$ such that the continuity equation holds:
$$ \frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (p_t v_t) = 0 $$
Samples are generated by solving the ODE:
$$ \frac{dz_t}{dt} = v(z_t, t), \quad z_0 \sim p_0 $$
This requires an ODE solver.

### 2.2. Defining Average Velocity
MeanFlow shifts the paradigm from modeling $v(z_t, t)$ to modeling the **average velocity** $u(z_t, r, t)$ over an interval $[r, t]$.

**Definition (Average Velocity):**
For a flow trajectory $z_\tau$ satisfying $\dot{z}_\tau = v(z_\tau, \tau)$, the average velocity between time $r$ and $t$ is the displacement normalized by the time duration:
$$ u(z_t, r, t) \triangleq \frac{1}{t-r} \int_r^t v(z_\tau, \tau) \, d\tau = \frac{z_t - z_r}{t - r} $$

Critically, $u(z_t, r, t)$ allows for 1-step generation. If we know $u(z_1, 0, 1)$, we can recover $z_1$ from $z_0$ directly:
$$ z_1 = z_0 + (1-0) \cdot u(z_1, 0, 1) $$
*(Note: The parameterization usually predicts $u$ at the current state $z_t$ looking backward or forward. The formulation above implies $z_t = z_r + (t-r)u$.)*

### 2.3. Derivation of the MeanFlow Identity
Directly training $u$ is difficult because the "ground truth" target requires integrating the path $z_\tau$, which is computationally expensive during training. The authors derive a differential constraint to avoid integration.

Starting from the rearranged definition:
$$ (t - r) u(z_t, r, t) = \int_r^t v(z_\tau, \tau) \, d\tau $$
Differentiate both sides with respect to the *current time* $t$. Note that $z_t$ is a function of $t$, so we use the total derivative $\frac{d}{dt}$.

**LHS:**
$$ \frac{d}{dt} \Big[ (t - r) u(z_t, r, t) \Big] = 1 \cdot u(z_t, r, t) + (t - r) \frac{d}{dt} u(z_t, r, t) $$

**RHS:**
Using the Leibniz integral rule (Fundamental Theorem of Calculus):
$$ \frac{d}{dt} \int_r^t v(z_\tau, \tau) \, d\tau = v(z_t, t) $$

Equating LHS and RHS yields the **MeanFlow Identity**:
$$ u(z_t, r, t) + (t - r) \frac{d}{dt} u(z_t, r, t) = v(z_t, t) $$

Rearranging for $v$:
$$ v(z_t, t) = u(z_t, r, t) + (t - r) \left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \nabla_z u \cdot \frac{dz_t}{dt} \right) $$
Since $\frac{dz_t}{dt} = v(z_t, t)$, this creates a circular dependency. However, in the context of *optimal transport* or linear paths used in training (where $v$ is determined by the coupling of $x_0$ and $x_1$), $v(z_t, t)$ is a known target vector.

The term $\frac{d}{dt} u$ is the **total derivative**, comprising the partial time derivative and the convective derivative:
$$ \frac{d}{dt} u(z_t, r, t) = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(z_t, r, t) + \nabla_z u(z_t, r, t) \cdot v(z_t, t) $$

## 3. Training Methodology

### 3.1. Loss Function
The neural network $u_\theta(z, r, t)$ parameterizes the average velocity. The training objective minimizes the violation of the MeanFlow Identity.

We construct a "predicted instantaneous velocity" $V_\theta$ from the network output:
$$ V_\theta(z_t, r, t) = u_\theta(z_t, r, t) + (t - r) \underbrace{\left( \frac{\partial u_\theta}{\partial t} + \nabla_z u_\theta \cdot v_{\text{target}}(z_t, t) \right)}_{\text{Total Derivative Approx}} $$

**The Loss:**
$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t, r, x_0, x_1} \left[ \left\| V_\theta(z_t, r, t) - v_{\text{target}}(z_t, t) \right\|^2 \right] $$

Where:
*   $x_0 \sim p_0, x_1 \sim p_1$.
*   $t \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 1], r \sim \mathcal{U}[0, t]$.
*   $z_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1$ (assuming Conditional Flow Matching / Linear Interpolant).
*   $v_{\text{target}}(z_t, t) = x_1 - x_0$.

### 3.2. Jacobian-Vector Product (JVP)
The term $\nabla_z u_\theta \cdot v_{\text{target}}$ requires computing the product of the Jacobian of the network output w.r.t. input $z$ and the vector $v_{\text{target}}$.
*   Calculating the full Jacobian is $O(D^2)$, but the **Jacobian-Vector Product (JVP)** can be computed in $O(D)$ (similar cost to a forward pass) using forward-mode automatic differentiation.
*   Frameworks like JAX (`jax.jvp`) or PyTorch (`torch.func.jvp`) support this efficiently.

### 3.3. Algorithm
1.  **Sample** times $r, t$ and data pair $(x_0, x_1)$.
2.  **Interpolate** $z_t$ and calculate target $v = x_1 - x_0$.
3.  **Forward Pass**: Compute $u_\theta(z_t, r, t)$.
4.  **JVP Pass**: Compute $\dot{u}_\theta = \text{JVP}(u_\theta, (z_t, t), (v, 1))$.
    *   Note: The derivative w.r.t time $t$ is also needed. The tangent vector is $(v, 1)$ corresponding to $(\dot{z}, \dot{t})$.
5.  **Reconstruct**: $V_{pred} = u_\theta + (t-r)\dot{u}_\theta$.
6.  **Backprop**: Minimize MSE between $V_{pred}$ and $v$.

## 4. Key Properties & Innovations

### 4.1. Self-Contained One-Step Model
Unlike Consistency Models (CM) or Rectified Flow, which often rely on distillation from a pre-trained teacher or iterative refinement, MeanFlow is trained **from scratch**. The identity provides a theoretically sound target ($v_{target}$) that exists without a teacher network.

### 4.2. Guidance Handling
In standard diffusion, Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) modifies the score: $\tilde{\epsilon} = (1+w)\epsilon_{cond} - w\epsilon_{uncond}$. This increases NFE.
MeanFlow incorporates guidance **into the target field**.
*   Let the target velocity be $v_{cfg} = (1+w)v_c - w v_u$.
*   MeanFlow learns a guided average velocity $u_{cfg}$ satisfying the identity w.r.t $v_{cfg}$.
*   **Result:** At inference time, a single evaluation of the guided network yields the guided sample. No extra computation is needed.

### 4.3. Stability vs. Efficiency Trade-off
The inclusion of the JVP in the loss creates a **second-order** dependency (gradients of gradients). While computationally heavier (roughly 2-3x cost of standard FM training per step), it is far cheaper than trajectory integration used in other methods.

## 5. Technical Critique

### 5.1. Strengths
*   **Mathematical Elegance**: The derivation from the fundamental theorem of calculus is clean and avoids the heuristic approximations found in "Shortcut Models".
*   **Efficiency**: 1-NFE generation is achieved directly.
*   **Performance**: The reported FID (3.43) is exceptionally strong for a scratch-trained one-step model, beating earlier Consistency Models.

### 5.2. Weaknesses / Subtleties
*   **Training Cost**: Computing the JVP requires a forward-mode AD pass, increasing training memory and time.
*   **Optimization Difficulty**: The loss involves the derivative of the network output. This can lead to high-frequency noise or instability (as noted in community implementations) because the optimizer is effectively pushing the *slope* of the function, not just the value.
*   **Boundary Conditions**: As $t \to r$, the term $(t-r)\dot{u}$ vanishes, and $u \to v$. The loss naturally degenerates to standard Flow Matching at $t=r$, ensuring consistency.

## 6. Conclusion
MeanFlow (arXiv:2505.13447) represents a maturing of one-step generative modeling. By identifying the differential relationship between average and instantaneous velocities, it formulates a tractable regression problem that bypasses the need for trajectory integration. It is a pivotal technique for real-time generative applications requiring high fidelity.
